The History of Toronto Slang

Where it came from, which neighbourhoods actually built it, how linguists study it today, and how a very local vocabulary ended up on every phone in North America.

Every generation of Toronto teenagers thinks the slang they're speaking just appeared out of nowhere — a group chat invention, a TikTok sound, a Drake lyric. It didn't. What gets called "Toronto slang," "6ix talk" or, on this site, "Toronto mans" language has a real, traceable history: specific neighbourhoods, specific immigration waves, and — as of the last decade — a specific academic name. Linguists call it Multicultural Toronto English, or MTE.

Where it developed: the inner suburbs, not downtown

The popular image of Toronto slang is downtown and glossy — Drake, OVO, the CN Tower on a hoodie. But by every account from the people who actually study it, the vocabulary itself was built somewhere else: in Toronto's inner suburbs, particularly Jane and Finch, Rexdale, and Malvern and other pockets of Scarborough. These were — and still are — dense, working-class, heavily immigrant neighbourhoods, built up through waves of post-war apartment construction that housed newcomers from all over the world within walking distance of each other.

That density is the whole story. When kids from Jamaican, Somali, Guyanese, Trinidadian, Vietnamese, South Asian and Ghanaian households all went to the same schools and played on the same courts, their languages didn't stay separate. They blended into a shared way of speaking that belonged to the neighbourhood, not to any one home country.

How it developed: two big immigration waves

Two migration waves in particular did the heavy lifting.

The first was Caribbean, and mostly Jamaican, immigration through the 1960s and 1970s, which picked up further through the 1980s as Toronto's Jamaican community grew and concentrated in the Jane-Finch and Rexdale corridors. Jamaican Patois — the English-based creole spoken across Jamaica — became the single biggest source of vocabulary in what would become Toronto slang: words like mans, man dem, wagwan, ahlie and bare all trace back to Patois.

The second wave was Somali, and it was sudden. Somalia's civil war in the early 1990s produced a refugee crisis, and Toronto — specifically those same northwest neighbourhoods — became a major resettlement destination. Refugee claims from Somalia jumped from roughly 31 in 1985 to over 3,500 in just the first half of 1991. A large, young Somali community landed in the same schools and blocks as the Jamaican community that had arrived a generation earlier, and Somali and Arabic vocabulary — words like wallahi — layered directly on top of the Patois-influenced English already spoken there.

Add in Guyanese and Trinidadian Creole, South Asian languages, and later waves of newcomers, and you get what linguists call a multiethnolect: a way of speaking, native to a neighbourhood rather than to any single ethnic group, that emerges specifically from young people growing up multilingual in dense immigrant communities. It is not "Jamaican slang used by everyone" or "Somali slang used by everyone" — it's a genuinely new, shared dialect that happens to draw most heavily from Patois, with real contributions from Somali, Arabic, and other languages spoken on the same streets.

Why it developed: what linguists actually call this

This isn't just internet folk linguistics — it's an active academic field. Dr. Derek Denis, a sociolinguist at the University of Toronto Mississauga, coined and researches the term Multicultural Toronto English (MTE), directly modelling the concept on earlier research into Multicultural London English (MLE) — a comparable multiethnolect that emerged in London for almost identical reasons: dense, multilingual, working-class immigrant neighbourhoods producing a shared new way of speaking among the kids who grew up there.

Denis and his research students have published peer-reviewed work studying MTE's vowel sounds, its grammar, and specific features like the pronoun-like use of "mans" (his paper is literally titled "A note on mans in Toronto"). He's also given a TED Talk, "Slang in the Six: Cultural Implications of Toronto Slang," on exactly this subject, and the Canadian Language Museum has run an exhibit and published A Dictionary of English in Multicultural Toronto documenting the same vocabulary from a different angle.

The short version of "why": multiethnolects like MTE develop wherever you get enough linguistic density and enough second-generation kids who need a way of speaking that's theirs — distinct from their parents' first language, distinct from "standard" Canadian English, and shared with the specific, multicultural neighbourhood they're actually from. It's not random. It's what happens, linguistically, almost anywhere multiple immigrant communities share the same streets for long enough.

From the block to global: Drake, OVO, and what got lost in translation

For decades, this vocabulary stayed almost entirely local — spoken in specific schools and specific neighbourhoods, invisible to most of Toronto let alone the rest of the world. That changed through the 2010s, largely through Drake and the broader Toronto rap scene (Toronto artist Jimmy Prime is widely credited with popularizing "the 6ix" specifically before Drake's Views From The 6 made it global). As Drake's music dominated globally, the vocabulary — Patois-rooted words, Somali and Arabic terms, Toronto-specific geography slang — went with it, first through music, then through social media, where the accent and vocabulary spread far beyond anyone who'd ever set foot in Rexdale or Jane-Finch.

That globalization is genuinely complicated, and worth being honest about. Coverage like The Walrus's reporting on "the Toronto accent" and the Globe and Mail's coverage of patois going mainstream both describe real tension inside the communities that built this vocabulary: pride at seeing their language go global, mixed with discomfort at watching words that once marked them as outsiders — sometimes mocked, sometimes over-policed — get picked up, flattened, and monetized by people with no connection to Jane-Finch, Rexdale, or the Jamaican and Somali diasporas who actually built it. Linguists and cultural critics have described this pattern as a kind of commodification: the culture spreads, but the credit and context often don't travel with it.

That history is exactly why this site tries to note where each word actually comes from, not just what it currently means — see the full dictionary for term-by-term origins, and the about section for how this project was researched.

Where it's headed

MTE keeps changing, the way any living dialect does — new words arrive with new waves of immigration (Toronto's more recent growth includes large South Asian, Filipino, and other communities whose linguistic contributions are still being documented), old words fall out of fashion, and social media now spreads new terms citywide in weeks instead of years. Academic interest is also still growing: Denis's lab continues publishing new MTE research, and it's increasingly taught and cited as a case study in how multiethnolects form.

What hasn't changed is the basic mechanism: put enough different languages on the same block for long enough, and the kids who grow up there will build something new out of all of it. Toronto did that. It's still doing it.
