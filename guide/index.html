History & Origins

The History of Toronto

How a fort on Lake Ontario became Canada's largest city — founding, war, railways, amalgamation, and the immigration waves that reshaped it.

Every Toronto Nickname, Explained

Toronto has accumulated more nicknames than most cities its size — some affectionate, some originally insults the city decided to keep anyway.

Toronto's Indigenous History

Toronto's own history — and even its name — starts long before York was founded in 1793. The land has been home to Indigenous peoples for over 10,000 years, and "Toronto" itself comes from a Mohawk word for a place well north of the modern city.

The Avro Arrow

Built in Malton, just outside Toronto, the CF-105 Arrow was one of the most advanced jet interceptors in the world — until the government cancelled it without warning on a single February afternoon in 1959.

The Railways That Built Toronto

Two rival 19th-century railway companies turned Toronto into central Canada's transport hub — one of them briefly ran the longest railway system in the world.

The Chinese Head Tax

Between 1885 and 1947, federal law imposed a punishing entry tax on Chinese immigrants and then banned Chinese immigration outright — a history Toronto's Chinese-Canadian community lived through directly, and one Canada formally apologized for in 2006.

The Great Fire of Toronto, 1904

On the night of April 19, 1904, a fire that started in a necktie factory burned through Toronto's entire wholesale and warehouse district — and rewrote the city's fire code.

The 1979 Mississauga Train Derailment

A CP Rail freight train carrying chlorine and propane derailed just before midnight on November 10, 1979 — triggering what was then the largest peacetime evacuation in North American history, with no fatalities.

COVID-19 in Toronto

Canada's pandemic began in Toronto, when a man returning from Wuhan was confirmed as the country's first case in January 2020. The city's state of emergency lasted 777 days.

The Eaton's Annex Fire

Months after the Eaton Centre opened, fire tore through the neighbouring Eaton's Annex building — badly damaging a historic downtown church and coming within reach of Toronto's newest landmark.

The Toronto Necropolis

Toronto's Necropolis cemetery in Cabbagetown holds the reburied remains of 1837 Rebellion figures William Lyon Mackenzie and Samuel Lount, alongside generations of the city's dead.

Sunnyside Amusement Park

Sunnyside opened in 1922 as a free lakeside amusement park nicknamed the 'Poor Man's Riviera,' and most of it was demolished in the 1950s to build the Gardiner Expressway.

John Graves Simcoe

Upper Canada's first Lieutenant Governor founded York in 1793 as a defensible, temporary capital, choosing the site primarily for military reasons rather than any long-term vision of the city Toronto would become.

Elizabeth Simcoe

Elizabeth Simcoe, wife of Upper Canada's first Lieutenant Governor, kept a detailed diary and sketchbook of early York that remains one of the richest surviving records of the town's founding years, and gave the Scarborough Bluffs their name.

Peter Russell

Peter Russell governed Upper Canada as administrator after Simcoe's departure, expanding early York while personally enslaving people even as the province's own new anti-slavery law was meant to be phasing the practice out.

The Family Compact

A tightly interconnected group of officials, judges and merchants known as the Family Compact controlled Upper Canada's government and economy from York for decades, until the grievances they provoked helped spark the 1837 Rebellion.

The Ward

For nearly a century, the crowded downtown district known as the Ward served as Toronto's first stop for waves of Irish, Jewish, Italian, Chinese and Black newcomers, before the city demolished most of it for civic buildings and hospitals.

Muddy York

Before it was Toronto, York's unpaved streets and heavy clay soil turned to near-impassable mud after every rain, giving the young town a nickname that outlasted the conditions that earned it.

The 1999 Snow Army

After a brutal series of snowstorms buried the city in January 1999, Mayor Mel Lastman formally requested Canadian Armed Forces help clearing snow — a decision that made Toronto a national punchline for years.

The Yonge Street 'Longest Street' Myth

For decades, Guinness World Records listed Yonge Street as the world's longest street at 1,896 kilometres — a figure that only worked by counting a provincial highway as part of the same road.

Scadding Cabin

Built around 1794 for John Graves Simcoe's estate manager, Scadding Cabin is the oldest surviving structure in Toronto, relocated to the CNE grounds in the 1870s to save it from demolition.

The Noronic Fire

A fire tore through the passenger ship SS Noronic while it was docked in Toronto Harbour overnight in September 1949, killing at least 118 people in the deadliest single disaster in the city's history.

Mount Pleasant Cemetery

Opened in 1876 as a rural-style landscaped cemetery, Mount Pleasant became the resting place for generations of Toronto's most prominent figures, and one of the city's most significant tree collections.

Fort Rouillé

Built by the French in 1750 and burned by its own garrison in 1759, Fort Rouillé predates the founding of York by more than four decades, on land now inside the CNE grounds.

The Toronto Purchase

The 1787 and 1805 agreements by which the British Crown acquired the land underlying Toronto from the Mississaugas of the Credit were only formally settled, with a $145-million payment, in 2010.

The 2003 Blackout

A cascading grid failure that started outside Cleveland reached Toronto within minutes on August 14, 2003, halting the subway mid-run and leaving officers directing gridlocked intersections by hand.

The 1918 Flu Pandemic in Toronto

Toronto's medical officer of health closed schools, theatres and churches within weeks of the virus arriving in the fall of 1918, in a pandemic that killed healthy young adults at an unusual rate.

Neighbourhoods & Suburbs

Toronto Neighbourhoods Guide

A general guide to Toronto's neighbourhoods — where downtown actually is, what Kensington Market and the Distillery District are like, and how the inner suburbs fit together. (For the slang side of specific neighbourhoods, see our dedicated page on that.)

Toronto's Ethnic Enclaves

Beyond the general neighbourhood map, Toronto has a specific set of streets that function as living cultural districts — three separate Chinatowns, a Greektown that was once the largest in North America, and more.

Scarborough

From a rural township of 3,000 people to a district that tripled its population between 1955 and 1970 — the deep history behind the part of the city locals just call "Scarbs."

Etobicoke

From a rural township incorporated in 1850 to a postwar suburb that grew from under 40,000 people to over 200,000 in two decades — Toronto's affluent-to-working-class west end former city.

North York

Almost single-handedly reshaped by one 25-year mayor, North York built its own downtown along Yonge Street — complete with its own city hall, square and skyline — before merging into Toronto in 1998.

Mississauga

In 1975 it had 235,000 people. Under one mayor's 36-year tenure, it grew into Canada's sixth-largest city — the fuller story behind the place Toronto slang just calls "Sauga."

Brampton

Once North America's largest flower-growing business, later one of the fastest-growing cities in Canada and now home to one of the largest Punjabi and Sikh communities outside South Asia.

Vaughan

Canada's largest theme park put Vaughan on the map in 1981. It took until 2017 for the subway — and a real downtown — to catch up.

York

The smallest and most densely working-class of the six municipalities that merged into Toronto in 1998 — home to a former Kodak film plant and one of the city's most enduring Italian-Canadian communities.

East York

Formed from the 1967 merger of a working-class township and the town of Leaside, East York packed one of the country's densest high-rise communities into its small footprint before merging into Toronto in 1998.

Markham

A 19th-century farming village named for an Archbishop of York became Canada's self-declared "high-tech capital" and one of its most ethnically diverse cities, largely within a single generation.

Oakville

Founded in 1827 by a colonel who built his fortune on white oak timber, Oakville grew from a Lake Ontario shipbuilding port into one of the most affluent suburbs in the Greater Toronto Area.

Durham Region & Oshawa

East of Toronto, Durham Region's largest city built its entire identity around one company — the McLaughlin carriage works that became General Motors of Canada, earning Oshawa the nickname "Canada's Motor City."

Richmond Hill

Built up as a stop along Yonge Street for farmers heading to Toronto's markets, Richmond Hill has more recently become one of the largest centres of Persian-Canadian community life outside Iran.

Ajax

Most Ontario towns are named after English hometowns or local landowners. Ajax is named after a Royal Navy warship — and its streets are named after that ship's actual crew.

Whitby

Long before it was a Toronto commuter suburb, Whitby was a serious 19th-century grain-shipping port — ranked third among all lake ports in Ontario for U.S. exports.

Pickering

When Pickering Nuclear Generating Station came online in the early 1970s, it was the largest nuclear power plant in the world — and it still supplies roughly a tenth of Ontario's electricity today.

Burlington

Most GTA-area towns trace back to a British colonial land grant. Burlington traces back to Mohawk war chief Joseph Brant, who settled the land the city still celebrates him for every August.

Milton

A 19th-century mill town named after an English poet became, for over a decade, the fastest-growing municipality in the entire country — with the Niagara Escarpment as its scenic backdrop.

Newmarket

While most of the GTA's founding stories involve British colonial land grants or war-hero honours, Newmarket was founded by pacifist Quaker families who crossed the border specifically to avoid fighting in the American Revolution.

Kensington Market

Kensington Market has changed its dominant culture at least four times in a century — Jewish in the 1920s, then Portuguese, Italian, Caribbean and Asian in succession — without ever losing its street-market character.

The Junction

A railway and stockyard neighbourhood that banned alcohol sales in 1904 to curb rowdy factory workers — and stayed officially dry for 94 years, surviving four separate failed attempts to overturn it.

Yorkville

In the 1960s, Yorkville's cheap Victorian rooming houses made it Toronto's folk-music and counterculture capital. Today the same blocks hold some of the most expensive retail real estate in the country.

Liberty Village

Liberty Village takes its name from the street walked by prisoners released from two 19th-century institutions that once stood there — a grim origin for one of Toronto's most redeveloped condo-and-office neighbourhoods.

The Beaches

What started as Ashbridge family farmland became a lakeside cottage colony, then a boardwalk neighbourhood — and the source of one of Toronto's longest-running local arguments: is it 'the Beach' or 'the Beaches'?

Cabbagetown

Irish famine immigrants who settled this east-downtown neighbourhood in the 1840s reportedly grew cabbages in their front yards, giving it a name that stuck through slum decades and a Victorian-house revival.

Leslieville

Named for a 19th-century nurseryman, Leslieville spent a century as a working-class brickmaking district before becoming one of Toronto's most-renovated stretches of Queen Street East.

Regent Park

Built starting in 1947 on land planners called one of Toronto's worst slums, Regent Park was Canada's first slum-clearance public housing project — and since 2005 it's been rebuilt again as a mixed-income neighbourhood.

Halton Hills

Georgetown grew up around a papermaking empire that ran for 130 years; Acton became a regional leather-tanning centre. In 1974 the two, plus surrounding farmland, were merged into one town.

Aurora

A single storefront at Yonge and Wellington grew into a crossroads hamlet called Machell's Corners — until a postmaster renamed it for the goddess of the dawn, and a railway turned it into a real town.

King Township

Unlike its fast-urbanizing York Region neighbours, King Township deliberately stayed rural — protected by the Oak Ridges Moraine and the Greenbelt, and built its modern identity around country estates and equestrian farms.

Uxbridge

Quaker settlers built Uxbridge's first mill in 1808. Two centuries later, the town has rebranded around a network of over 200 kilometres of trails through the Oak Ridges Moraine.

Caledon

More than any other GTA municipality, Caledon's identity is inseparable from its landscape — the Niagara Escarpment runs straight through it, and the town has deliberately kept most of its land rural.

Clarington

Formed from Clarke and Darlington townships in 1974, Clarington is home to a last-largely-intact WWII POW camp designated a National Historic Site — and the nuclear station that supplies a fifth of Ontario's power.

Corso Italia

As College Street's Little Italy grew more commercial, Italian Toronto shifted north to St. Clair Avenue West — a strip that got its name, and its identity, cemented by one unforgettable night in 1982.

Greektown, the Danforth

Toronto's Greek community started downtown, not on the Danforth — and moved east partly in the aftermath of a documented riot against Greek-owned businesses before becoming, by the 1980s, the largest Greektown in North America.

Little India, Gerrard Street

In 1972, one businessman bought a struggling theatre and started screening Bollywood films exclusively — and the commercial spillover became one of the largest South Asian marketplaces in North America.

Roncesvalles

The avenue takes its name from a Spanish battle an early settler fought in. Its identity today comes from somewhere else entirely: the Polish immigrants and refugees who arrived after the Second World War.

Forest Hill

Named for a summer estate at Eglinton and Old Forest Hill Road, this neighbourhood stayed a self-governing village longer than almost anywhere else in the city, before becoming one of its wealthiest addresses.

Rosedale

A sheriff's wife named her family's estate for the wild roses growing on it. When the land was subdivided in 1864, her habitual riding routes reportedly became some of the first curving suburban streets in Canada.

Bloor West Village

A group of merchants with no reliable way to fund streetscape improvements invented a solution in 1970 — and Toronto's answer to a local funding problem became a model copied by cities around the world.

Swansea

Self-governing from 1926 until Toronto absorbed it against many residents' wishes, Swansea takes its name from a Welsh town its early settlers thought its rolling hills resembled.

Whitchurch-Stouffville

A Pennsylvania Mennonite built a sawmill on Duffin's Creek in 1824. The hamlet that grew around it eventually merged with its surrounding township in 1971 to form one of York Region's fastest-changing municipalities.

East Gwillimbury

A town of historic hamlets near Lake Simcoe became, almost overnight, the fastest-growing municipality of its size in the entire country — with its population expected to triple by 2051.

Bradford West Gwillimbury

What was once dismissed as unusable swampland became, after an early-20th-century drainage project, one of the most productive farmland patches in Canada — a $450-million-a-year vegetable operation still called "muck farming."

Scugog and Port Perry

Peter Perry bought 40 acres and opened a waterfront trading post in 1840, naming the settlement Scugog Village. When he died the following decade, the community renamed itself after him.

Brock Township

A former British prime minister's name ended up on a small Ontario village almost by accident — one of three historic communities merged into Brock Township when Durham Region was created.

Orangeville

A Connecticut-born mill owner named Orange Lawrence settled the land in 1844 and gave the town his first name. Two decades later, it became the seat of Ontario's newest county.

Chinatown

Toronto's downtown Chinatown around Spadina Avenue and Dundas Street survived urban renewal plans in the 1960s that would have demolished it for a new city hall and expressway.

Chinatown East

As rising rents pushed businesses out of the original Spadina-area Chinatown from the 1970s onward, a second Chinatown developed along Gerrard and Broadview, giving Toronto's Chinese community a second commercial anchor.

The Bridle Path

Developed starting in the 1920s on former farmland, the Bridle Path became synonymous with extreme wealth in Toronto, its name preserving the equestrian trails that once wound through the estate lots.

Lawrence Park

Developed starting in 1909 as a deliberately upscale garden suburb, Lawrence Park's original restrictive covenants shaped it into one of midtown Toronto's most affluent, architecturally consistent neighbourhoods.

Leaside

The Canadian Northern Railway laid out Leaside in 1913 as a fully planned industrial town with its own rail yards, years before enough houses existed to give it much of a population.

Deer Park

Deer Park takes its name from an actual deer herd kept on a 19th-century estate near St. Clair and Yonge, long since subdivided into one of midtown Toronto's older residential neighbourhoods.

Harbord Village

Squeezed between the University of Toronto and Kensington Market, Harbord Village organized in the 1970s to block university expansion plans that would have demolished much of its Victorian housing stock.

Scarborough Town Centre

Opened in 1973, Scarborough Town Centre was built as the commercial core of a planned civic centre meant to give the sprawling suburb a downtown of its own.

Agincourt

Named for a French battle by a homesick English settler, Agincourt grew from a small farming crossroads into a Scarborough neighbourhood with one of the city's most established Chinese-Canadian communities.

Malvern

Malvern was developed in the 1970s as one of the last large planned suburban communities built under Metro Toronto, on farmland in northeast Scarborough with little existing settlement to build around.

Rexdale

Developer Rex Heslop built Rexdale starting in the 1950s as a planned residential and industrial community in northern Etobicoke, giving the neighbourhood his own first name.

Weston Village

Weston grew up as an independent mill town on the Humber River in the early 19th century, incorporated as its own town, and wasn't absorbed into Metro Toronto until 1967.

Jane and Finch

Built rapidly in the 1960s and 70s around a cluster of apartment towers, Jane and Finch became one of Toronto's most talked-about neighbourhoods, and one of its most persistently mischaracterized.

Corktown

Corktown took its name from County Cork, Ireland, whose famine-era immigrants settled around the breweries and distilleries of Toronto's east end in the 19th century.

Little Jamaica

Caribbean immigrants built a corridor of reggae record shops, roti places, and hair salons along Eglinton Avenue West starting in the 1960s and 70s — a corridor a decade-plus of LRT construction badly disrupted.

Alexandra Park

Built in the 1960s as a low-rise public housing complex west of Kensington Market, Alexandra Park has spent the last decade being redeveloped into a mixed-income neighbourhood.

Moss Park

Named for a long-demolished 19th-century estate, Moss Park became a dense downtown-east neighbourhood defined by social housing, shelters, and, more recently, a supervised drug consumption site.

The West Don Lands

Long an industrial brownfield, the West Don Lands was rebuilt for Toronto's 2015 Pan American Games as an athletes' village, seeded with flood protection infrastructure decades in the making.

CityPlace

Built starting in the late 1990s on former railway lands next to the Rogers Centre, CityPlace became one of the largest and densest condominium developments in Canada.

St. James Town

Built through the 1960s as a cluster of high-rise apartment towers, St. James Town became one of the most densely populated neighbourhoods in Canada and a landing point for generations of new immigrants.

Flemingdon Park

Developed starting in the late 1950s on former farmland, Flemingdon Park was planned as a self-contained postwar community of apartment towers, and today is one of Toronto's most diverse immigrant neighbourhoods.

Thorncliffe Park

Built in the 1950s and 60s on the site of a former horse racing track, Thorncliffe Park became one of Toronto's most densely populated neighbourhoods and a major hub for South Asian immigrant life.

Ward's Island

Toronto tried to clear the Toronto Islands' cottage community twice in the 20th century — residents fought both attempts, and around 250 households still live year-round on Ward's and Algonquin Islands.

The Annex

Developed in the 1880s as an upscale suburb annexed into Toronto, the Annex's large Victorian and Romanesque houses were later subdivided into rooming houses as the University of Toronto grew next door.

Parkdale

Parkdale incorporated as its own village in 1878 with grand lakeside mansions, was annexed by Toronto in 1889, and cycled through decline and gentrification over the following century.

Alderwood

A working-class south Etobicoke community tied to the industrial belt along Toronto's western waterfront, Alderwood filled in with modest brick bungalows through the post-war housing boom.

Wychwood

A landscape painter founded a private artists' enclave in 1874, and a set of derelict TTC streetcar barns down the hill became one of the city's most-copied adaptive reuse projects.

Summerhill

The Canadian Pacific Railway built a grand terminal at Summerhill in 1916 with a clock tower modelled on Venice's Piazza San Marco — and after passenger rail moved on, the building became an LCBO.

Davisville

John Davis opened the area's first post office and pottery works in 1840, and eighty years later a land company turned his old holdings into a dense streetcar-and-subway suburb.

Moore Park

John Thomas Moore staked his own money on the Belt Line Railway to sell lots in his new subdivision — the line went bankrupt within two years, and Moore Park's growth stalled for a generation.

Long Branch

A 19th-century developer named his lakeside resort after the New Jersey seaside town he'd vacationed in — Long Branch stayed an independent village until amalgamating into Etobicoke in 1967.

Mimico

Mimico's name recalls the passenger pigeon flocks that once filled the area — a history that also includes a 19th-century lakeside asylum and decades as its own independent town.

New Toronto

Founded around 1890 as a planned industrial town on Lake Ontario, New Toronto stayed an independent municipality for decades before joining Etobicoke and, later, the amalgamated city.

Government & Civic Institutions

Toronto Public Library

Toronto runs the busiest urban public library system in the world — more circulation per capita than any other city, spread across 100 branches, and free to use for everyone.

Toronto's City Government

How Toronto is actually governed post-amalgamation — the mayor and city council system, the architecture of City Hall itself, and the mayoralty that briefly made the city a global punchline.

Queen's Park

Ontario's provincial legislature has run out of the same pink-sandstone Romanesque building since 1893 — a building whose 10.5 million bricks were made inside a Toronto prison.

Osgoode Hall

Home to Ontario's highest courts since 1832, Osgoode Hall is best known for a set of ornamental gates whose whole "built to stop cows" origin story a group of 1950s law students actually tried, and failed, to disprove.

The LCBO

Ontario's government-run liquor retailer was created in 1927 to replace outright prohibition with something more controlled — and it took more than 40 years before shoppers could actually browse a shelf themselves.

PRESTO

Ontario's regional transit fare card started as a tiny 2007 pilot in Mississauga and grew into the single payment system linking the TTC, GO Transit and ten other agencies across the region.

Nathan Phillips Square

An international design competition drew more than 500 entries before a Finnish architect none of the judges had met in person won the commission for Toronto's modern City Hall and the square in front of it.

Toronto Hydro

Before 1911, electricity in Ontario was a private, for-profit business. A London, Ontario mayor named Adam Beck changed that — and Toronto Hydro grew out of what became the world's first publicly owned power authority.

The Founding of the Toronto Police

Toronto's police force was created the same year the city itself was incorporated — and spent its first 25 years under the direct, often politically compromised control of individual aldermen.

Bedford v. Canada

Three Toronto sex workers challenged Canada's prostitution laws as unconstitutional. The case's appeal was argued and decided at Osgoode Hall — a distinct legal chapter from the building's general history.

Wrongful Convictions and Ontario's Innocence Movement

The wrongful convictions of Guy Paul Morin and Steven Truscott exposed systemic failures in Ontario's justice system — and directly led to the founding of what's now Innocence Canada.

The Special Investigations Unit

Ontario's Special Investigations Unit investigates Toronto police and every other police service in the province when someone is killed or seriously injured — a model Ontario pioneered in 1990 and has repeatedly reformed since.

David Crombie

Elected in 1972 as part of a citizen-activist reform wave, Crombie brought in a height limit that stopped a wave of high-rise redevelopment — and built the housing project still cited as a template for the city.

Mel Lastman

A furniture-store owner who governed North York for a quarter century became the first mayor of the newly amalgamated City of Toronto in 1998 — and became a national punchline the following January for calling in the military to clear snow.

Rob Ford's Mayoralty

Rob Ford served as Toronto's mayor from 2010 to 2014, through a drug-use scandal that made international news, a council vote to strip most of his powers, and a diagnosis that ended his re-election campaign.

John Tory

John Tory served two terms building transit and poverty-reduction programs — then resigned within a week of admitting an extramarital relationship with a former staffer.

Olivia Chow

Chow immigrated to Toronto at 13, was elected to the school board at 28, and spent decades in municipal and federal politics before winning the mayoralty in a 2023 by-election with housing as her central promise.

Toronto's Ward Structure

The province cut Toronto's council from a planned 47 wards to 25, mid-campaign, in 2018. A court called it unconstitutional. A higher court reversed that. The 25-ward map has run the city ever since.

The Dundas Street Renaming Debate

Toronto City Council voted in 2021 to rename Dundas Street over its namesake's historical role delaying the abolition of the slave trade, then reversed the decision in 2024 citing the cost of the change.

Metro Toronto

The Municipality of Metropolitan Toronto formed in 1954 to coordinate services like water and roads across the city and its surrounding suburbs, decades before full amalgamation in 1998.

Toronto's Laneway Housing Reform

Toronto legalized laneway suites citywide in 2018, opening up roughly 3,000 back-lane lots to small secondary homes as one response to the city's housing shortage.

David Miller

Mayor from 2003 to 2010, David Miller built his political identity on opposing a bridge to the Toronto Island Airport and later faced a lengthy municipal workers' strike over garbage collection.

Barbara Hall

Barbara Hall served as mayor of the old City of Toronto from 1994 to 1997, the last mayor before provincial amalgamation folded the city and five surrounding municipalities into one.

Landmarks & Architecture

Toronto Landmarks

The buildings and sites that define Toronto's skyline and street level — from the CN Tower down to a 19th-century castle in midtown.

Toronto Architecture

A style found almost nowhere else — the Victorian bay-and-gable rowhouse — sitting a short walk from one of the tallest, densest condo skylines in North America.

Union Station

Toronto's grand 1927 train hall is the single busiest piece of transportation infrastructure in the country — over a quarter million people a day pass through it, more than through Pearson Airport.

The Bloor Viaduct

Officially the Prince Edward Viaduct, this 1918 bridge across the Don Valley was engineered with a lower deck for a subway line that wouldn't be built for another 48 years — one of the most farsighted pieces of infrastructure planning in Toronto's history.

The R.C. Harris Water Treatment Plant

Toronto's drinking water is filtered inside a 1941 Art Deco building so ornate it earned the nickname "Palace of Purification" — and inspired the same author who wrote about the Bloor Viaduct.

Toronto's Historic Cemeteries

Mount Pleasant Cemetery is a designated National Historic Site laid out like a 19th-century garden — and the final resting place of the same insulin co-discoverer covered elsewhere in this guide.

Casa Loma

Built by a Toronto financier who went spectacularly bankrupt within a decade, Casa Loma spent World War II hiding a genuine military secret behind a one-dollar padlock.

The Royal Ontario Museum

Canada's largest museum added a jagged glass-and-metal crystal to its 1914 building in 2007 — one of the most polarizing pieces of architecture in Toronto, and one of its most effective.

The CN Tower

Built in 40 months using a giant concrete mold that climbed itself upward, the CN Tower held the world record for tallest freestanding structure from 1975 until Dubai's Burj Khalifa passed it in 2007.

Massey Hall

Built by an industrialist in memory of his son, Massey Hall has hosted everyone from the 1953 "greatest jazz concert ever played" to a $184-million, three-year renovation that finished in 2021.

Fort York

The 1793 fort that gave the young town of York its defence saw its gunpowder magazine detonated in 1813 to keep it out of American hands — killing dozens, including the American general leading the attack.

Spadina Museum

Banker James Austin built his family's estate on the hill overlooking downtown in 1866. Three generations of Austins lived there until 1982 — right next to the castle that came decades later.

Colborne Lodge

Toronto's first professional architect built himself a cottage on a wooded lakeshore lot in 1837 — then gave the entire estate to the city, on the condition he could live in it until he died.

Mackenzie House

William Lyon Mackenzie led an armed rebellion against Toronto's ruling elite in 1837 and was forced to flee the country. Two decades later, his supporters bought him this house to retire in.

Campbell House Museum

The oldest surviving building from the original Town of York was about to be demolished in the 1970s — until a group of lawyers physically moved it, on rollers, to a new corner downtown.

Todmorden Mills

What began as a 1795 sawmill on the Don River grew into a small industrial hamlet — a brewery, distillery and paper mill — before becoming a heritage site combining museum galleries with a 9-hectare wildflower preserve.

Black Creek Pioneer Village

Rather than preserve one historic house, this site rescued dozens — reassembling threatened 19th-century buildings from across Ontario into a single recreated 1860s village.

Gibson House Museum

Surveyor David Gibson took part in the 1837 Rebellion alongside William Lyon Mackenzie — and paid for it when government forces burned his farmhouse. He rebuilt on the same land after his pardon.

The Toronto-Dominion Centre

When black steel-and-glass towers replaced a Beaux-Arts bank headquarters in 1967, they permanently changed what a Toronto skyscraper was supposed to look like — designed by the same architect behind Manhattan's Seagram Building.

Commerce Court

The Canadian Bank of Commerce's 1931 tower claimed the tallest-building title in the British Empire — a record it had actually just taken from the hotel next door, and would lose again three decades later.

First Canadian Place

Completed in 1975, this BMO-headquartered tower has held the title of Canada's tallest building for longer than most Torontonians have been alive.

Scotia Plaza

Rather than demolish the Bank of Nova Scotia's heritage-designated 1951 building, developers built one of Toronto's tallest towers directly onto and around it.

Aura at College Park

The last building in a residential complex begun decades earlier became, on completion in 2014, the tallest residential tower in the country — a very different kind of record from the bank-owned office towers nearby.

The Elgin and Winter Garden Theatre Centre

Two full-sized vaudeville theatres, stacked one above the other, closed for decades before a restoration reopened them on the exact anniversary of their original opening night.

The John Street Roundhouse

Built starting in 1929 to service Canadian Pacific Railway locomotives, the John Street Roundhouse survived decades of disuse near the CN Tower before being restored as a railway museum and brewery site.

The Confederation Life Building

Built in the 1890s as headquarters for the Confederation Life insurance company, the Romanesque Revival building on Richmond Street survived its namesake company's 1994 collapse and remains a downtown landmark.

The Aga Khan Museum

Opened in 2014 on the site of a former CBC property in North York, the Aga Khan Museum became North America's first museum dedicated exclusively to Islamic art and culture.

The Princes' Gates

Built in 1927 for Canada's diamond jubilee of Confederation and opened by two visiting British princes, the Princes' Gates remain the ceremonial entrance to Exhibition Place.

The Automotive Building

Built in 1929 at Exhibition Place to display the latest cars to the public, the Automotive Building's Art Deco design made it one of the CNE grounds' most architecturally significant structures.

Fort York Armoury

Built in 1935 next to the original Fort York, the armoury has served Toronto's militia units for generations and later doubled as a temporary refugee reception centre.

The Toronto Sign

Installed at Nathan Phillips Square for the 2015 Pan American Games as temporary signage, the giant illuminated TORONTO letters proved so popular the city made them a permanent, colour-changing landmark.

The Gooderham Building

Built in 1892 for distiller George Gooderham to fit a triangular downtown lot, the Gooderham Building is commonly called Toronto's Flatiron — and it opened years before New York's better-known building of the same shape.

The Royal Alexandra Theatre

Opened in 1907 and named for Queen Alexandra, the Royal Alex faced demolition by the 1960s before Ed Mirvish bought and restored it, making it Toronto's oldest working theatre.

The Grange

Built in 1817 by one of York's wealthiest families, The Grange was donated to found what became the Art Gallery of Ontario, and still stands attached to the modern museum.

The Fairmont Royal York

Built by the Canadian Pacific Railway across from Union Station, the Royal York opened in 1929 as one of the grandest hotels in the Commonwealth and has hosted royalty ever since.

The King Edward Hotel

Opened in 1903 by George Gooderham and designed by E.J. Lennox, the 'King Eddy' was Toronto's first luxury hotel and later hosted the Beatles and Ernest Hemingway.

Old City Hall

E.J. Lennox's 1899 Romanesque city hall served Toronto for over sixty years before Nathan Phillips Square opened — and Lennox, denied formal credit, carved his name into the stone himself.

St. Michael's Cathedral Basilica

Commissioned by Toronto's first Catholic bishop and completed in 1848, St. Michael's became the anchor institution for a city flooded with Irish famine migrants that same year.

Holy Blossom Temple

Founded in 1856 by English and German Jewish immigrants, Holy Blossom shifted from Orthodox roots into Canada's largest Reform congregation under Rabbi Barnett Brickner.

Metropolitan United Church

Built in the 1870s as the largest Methodist church in Canada, Metropolitan United survived a devastating 1928 fire and became one of the United Church of Canada's most visible downtown congregations.

Jami Mosque

Founded by the Muslim Society of Toronto in the 1950s, Jami Mosque was formally dedicated in 1969 after years of the community praying in rented halls and private homes.

St. James Cathedral

Founded in 1797, St. James is Toronto's oldest Anglican congregation — though its current Gothic Revival building, with a spire completed in 1874, dates only to after the Great Fire of 1849.

Little Trinity Church

Built five years before the current St. James Cathedral, Little Trinity is Toronto's oldest standing church building — funded in part by brewer and philanthropist Enoch Turner for Corktown's working-class Irish.

The Humber Bay Arch Bridge

Before 1994, the Martin Goodman Trail simply dead-ended at the mouth of the Humber River — a pedestrian bridge with tiered steel arches finally closed that gap.

The R.C. Harris Water Treatment Plant

Commissioner Roland Caldwell Harris built Toronto's east-end filtration plant with the ambition of a civic monument — earning it the nickname 'palace of purification' and a role in an Ondaatje novel.

The R.L. Hearn Generating Station

Once one of the largest coal-fired power plants in Canada, the Hearn stopped generating electricity in the early 1980s and has since become a recurring venue for large-scale arts events.

The Leaside Bridge

Opened in 1927, the Leaside Bridge carries Millwood Road across the Don Valley, solving a problem built into Leaside's original 1913 plan as an isolated industrial town.

The Luminous Veil

After decades of the Prince Edward Viaduct recording one of the highest bridge suicide tolls in North America, a public design competition produced a barrier that studies found effectively eliminated jumps.

Parks, Nature & Waterfront

Toronto Parks & Green Space

A car-free island park in Lake Ontario, a 400-acre park with an endangered oak savannah, and a converted brick quarry turned environmental centre — Toronto's green space is stranger and better than its skyline suggests.

Toronto Weather & Climate

Toronto runs a genuine humid continental climate — meaning real winters and real summers, not the mild in-between some people expect from a lake city.

Toronto's Waterfront

A former industrial shipping and rail strip along Lake Ontario, being rebuilt piece by piece into parks, beaches — including a pink-umbrella "urban beach" — and public space.

Toronto's Ravine System

Hidden beneath the street grid is the largest ravine system of any city in the world — 30 times the size of New York's Central Park, and within a 10-minute walk of nearly a third of Toronto residents.

Toronto's Worst Storms

Our general climate guide covers Toronto's normal weather. This is the abnormal side of it — a 1954 hurricane that reshaped the city's relationship to its own rivers, and a single year, 2013, that delivered a record flood and a historic ice storm five months apart.

The Toronto Island Ferries

Getting to the Toronto Islands has meant a ferry since the 19th century — the only way to reach North America's largest urban car-free residential community, and the site of Babe Ruth's first professional home run.

High Park

Architect John Howard gave Toronto its largest park in 1873 on the condition it stay as natural as possible — a decision that preserved a nationally rare oak savannah, and later made room for a gift of cherry trees from Tokyo.

Toronto Harbour

The Toronto Harbour Commissioners, formed in 1911, built both of the city's airports before the modern port authority took over — a single agency's fingerprints are on more of Toronto's infrastructure than most people realize.

The Don River's Flood History

Our ravines guide covers the Don Valley's parkland today. This is about the river's darker history: repeated floods, a century-old industrial channel that made them worse, and the $1.4 billion project built to finally fix it.

Tommy Thompson Park

The Leslie Street Spit was never meant to be a nature reserve — it was lakefill for a shipping expansion that container ships made obsolete. Nobody planted what grew there instead, and it's now a globally significant bird sanctuary.

Rouge National Urban Park

Canada's first National Urban Park pairs protected wilderness with roughly 4,700 acres of active farmland — a deliberately different model from a traditional national park, spanning Toronto, Markham, Pickering and Uxbridge.

Trinity Bellwoods Park

One of Toronto's busiest parks was a rival Anglican college campus for over 70 years. Almost nothing of the original buildings survives above ground today except its 1903 entrance gates.

The Beltline Trail

A speculative 1890s commuter railway went bankrupt before it even opened and shut down for good less than three years later. Nearly a century after that, its abandoned corridor became one of Toronto's best trails.

Riverdale Farm

Toronto's first zoo operated on this site for 80 years, housing elephants and hippos by Victorian standards that would be unthinkable today. When it closed in 1974, the city rebuilt the site as something entirely different: a working farm.

The Vale of Avoca

This midtown ravine carries the only stretch of Yellow Creek that isn't buried in storm sewers — and has been crossed by real-estate-driven bridges since 1888, long before it became a protected park.

The Toronto Zoo

A private citizen pitched Metro Toronto's council on a new zoo in 1963. Eleven years later, it opened on Rouge Park land in Scarborough — replacing the century-old Riverdale Zoo downtown.

Edwards Gardens

A Scottish miller's overgrown property became a wealthy businessman's private estate in 1944 — then, a decade later, a public park with one of the largest rockeries in the country.

Guild Park and Gardens

As Toronto tore down its old buildings through the mid-20th century, one collector saved their most striking pieces — columns, arches, facades — and arranged them into an open-air park on the Scarborough Bluffs.

Sunnybrook Park

Joseph and Alice Kilgour turned scrubland into a showcase farm with fox hunts and prize cattle. After Joseph's death, Alice gave the entire estate to Toronto.

Withrow Park

The City of Toronto bought the land for Withrow Park in 1910, giving the growing Riverdale neighbourhood a large public green space that still anchors the area's community life today.

Ashbridges Bay

Once a large natural marsh at the mouth of the Don River, Ashbridges Bay was drained and filled through the early 20th century to build Toronto's largest sewage treatment plant and its surrounding Port Lands.

Warden Woods Park

A forested Scarborough ravine along Taylor-Massey Creek, Warden Woods Park became a protected green corridor even as postwar development filled in the neighbourhoods around it.

Black Creek

Black Creek, a tributary of the Humber River, lent its name to both Black Creek Pioneer Village and the Jane and Finch area, two very different pieces of Toronto identity tied to the same waterway.

Berczy Park

Redesigned in 2017 around a fountain covered in sculpted dogs of every breed, Berczy Park turned a modest downtown square into one of Toronto's most Instagrammed public spaces.

James Gardens

Originally the private grounds of businessman Frederick Vibart Massey, James Gardens on the Humber River became a public formal garden after the city acquired it, prized for its landscaped flower beds and stonework.

Centennial Park

Built in the late 1960s on former gravel pit land to mark Canada's centennial, Centennial Park grew into one of Etobicoke's largest sports and recreation complexes.

The Humber Arboretum

Established in the 1970s as a partnership between Humber College and the Toronto region's conservation authority, the Humber Arboretum combines a public garden with an active horticultural teaching site.

Cedarvale Park

Cedarvale Park's ravine was slated to carry the Spadina Expressway before that project's 1971 cancellation preserved it as parkland, connecting to the city's beltline trail system instead of a highway.

G. Ross Lord Park

Built around a dam constructed after Hurricane Hazel to control flooding on the West Don River, G. Ross Lord Park combines flood infrastructure with a large multi-use recreational green space.

Chorley Park

Ontario's Lieutenant Governor lived in a grand chateau-style residence on a Rosedale ravine bluff for just over thirty years before the government demolished it in 1961 rather than keep paying to maintain it.

Kew Beach

Named by an English immigrant after London's Kew Gardens, Kew Beach began as a private lakeside garden before the city bought the land and opened it as public parkland in the 1880s.

Downsview Park

Once a Canadian Forces air base, Downsview closed to military use in the 1990s and was redeveloped as a large public park, with much of its remaining land still transitioning to new housing.

Allan Gardens

Donated to the city in 1858 by mayor George William Allan, Allan Gardens grew into a Victorian glass-domed conservatory that has outlasted repeated fires and the surrounding neighbourhood's decades of decline.

Evergreen Brick Works

The Don Valley Brick Works supplied clay bricks for much of Toronto's built city from 1889 to 1989, and its abandoned quarry and kiln buildings later became one of the city's best-known green redevelopment projects.

Dufferin Grove Park

Community organizers turned Dufferin Grove Park into a widely studied model of resident-led park programming, built around a bake oven, rink house, and farmers' market run largely by locals.

Riverdale Park

Riverdale Park East and West sit on opposite sides of the Don Valley Parkway, connected by pedestrian bridges, and together form one of the largest green spaces in Toronto's east end.

The Bentway

Opened in 2018 in the concrete undercroft of the Gardiner Expressway, the Bentway turned nearly two kilometres of dead highway-support space into a year-round public gathering place.

Sugar Beach

Opened in 2010 on the East Bayfront, Sugar Beach brought pink umbrellas and imported sand to a stretch of waterfront still overlooked by the Redpath Sugar refinery it's named for.

Cherry Beach

Once notorious for isolated police beatings that gave rise to the phrase 'Cherry Beach Express,' the Port Lands beach has since been redeveloped into a popular waterfront park and windsurfing spot.

HTO Park

Opened in 2007 on former parking lot land, HTO Park brought striped umbrellas and imported sand to Toronto's central waterfront, its name a nod to the H2O it deliberately doesn't let you swim in.

The Port Lands Flood Protection Project

A multi-billion-dollar project to give the Don River a new, naturalized mouth through the Port Lands removed a century-old flood risk and opened up hundreds of acres for redevelopment.

The Oak Ridges Moraine

A ridge of sand and gravel left by retreating glaciers roughly 12,000 years ago, the Oak Ridges Moraine became the centre of a major land-protection fight as GTA sprawl pushed north.

The Scarborough Bluffs' Erosion

The soft, layered sediment that makes the Scarborough Bluffs spectacular also makes them constantly unstable — a problem that reshaped shoreline policy after Hurricane Hazel in 1954.

Transit & Getting Around

Toronto Transit: The TTC

Canada's first subway, a streetcar network locals fought to save, and North America's largest underground pedestrian city — how Toronto actually gets around.

Toronto's Airports

Canada's busiest airport, and — a fraction of its size but a straight shot to the downtown core — the small island airport most first-time visitors don't know exists.

Cycling in Toronto

Toronto cyclists were lobbying City Hall for better infrastructure in 1896. The Bloor Street bike lane — arguably the city's flagship piece of cycling infrastructure — didn't open until 2016.

GO Transit

Where the TTC ends, GO Transit picks up — the regional rail and bus system that stitches Toronto to its surrounding commuter belt, running since a single experimental line in 1967.

The Gardiner Expressway & the DVP

The elevated highway locals call the Gardiner took 11 years to build, demolished a beloved amusement park to do it, and has been the subject of demolition debates almost since the day it opened.

The PATH

Toronto's underground pedestrian network connects 75+ buildings across 30 kilometres of climate-controlled tunnels — for decades the largest system of its kind on Earth, until Montreal's rival network recently edged it out.

TTC Subway Art

Toronto's subway runs an unusually ambitious public art program — none more striking than Museum Station's 2008 renovation, which turned structural support columns into a tour through world civilizations.

TTC Subway Fire & Safety History

A fatal 1995 collision on Line 1 remains the single most consequential safety incident in TTC history — reshaping signal technology and operating procedure years after the crash itself.

Line 2 (Bloor-Danforth Subway)

When Line 2 opened in 1966, the TTC discontinued the Bloor and Danforth streetcar routes on the very same day — the largest single cutback to Toronto's streetcar network since the original Yonge subway opened.

The Scarborough RT

The provincial government pushed the TTC to adopt an experimental, proprietary rail technology as an export showcase. Decades later, the same lack of adopters that made it a poor export made it nearly impossible to keep running.

Toronto's Streetcar Network

By the 1960s, most North American cities had scrapped their streetcars for buses. Toronto didn't — making the TTC the largest streetcar operator on the continent, almost by process of elimination.

The Eglinton Crosstown LRT

Construction began in 2011 on a line originally slated to open in 2020. It finally opened to riders in February 2026, after costs rose well beyond the original budget.

UP Express

The Union Pearson Express opened in 2015 as one of the most expensive airport rail links in North America — and it showed, in ridership numbers that fell for months until Metrolinx slashed the fare in 2016.

Bike Share Toronto

Toronto's bike-share system nearly died in 2013 when its Montreal-based operator couldn't repay a city loan. The Toronto Parking Authority took it over instead of shutting it down — and it's grown every year since.

Pearson International Airport's History

Toronto's main airport opened in 1939 on farmland near Malton, growing over decades into Canada's busiest airport before being renamed in 1984 for Nobel Peace Prize-winning prime minister Lester B. Pearson.

Billy Bishop Toronto City Airport

Toronto's island airport has operated since the 1930s, named for a First World War flying ace, and became a genuine downtown transit option after a regional airline began scheduled jet service in 2006.

The Toronto Railway Company

A private company ran Toronto's streetcars from 1891 to 1921 under a 30-year franchise, refusing to extend lines into new suburbs it couldn't profit from — a fight that led the city to take transit public.

The Ontario Line

The Ontario Line, a provincially-led subway project connecting Ontario Place to the Ontario Science Centre, emerged after the province cancelled a previous relief line plan the city had spent years developing.

The Bloor Street Bike Lane Pilot

A 2016 pilot project installed protected bike lanes on a stretch of Bloor Street after decades of advocacy — and the debate over making them permanent, and extending them, has continued ever since.

Line 1: The Yonge-University Subway

Toronto's Yonge subway opened on March 30, 1954 as the first subway line in Canada, backed by a rare municipal referendum in which voters approved paying for it themselves.

Sports

Toronto Sports Culture

Toronto is one of a handful of cities with a major team in every big North American league — hockey, basketball, baseball, football, and soccer, all headquartered a few kilometres apart downtown.

Rogers Centre (SkyDome)

When it opened in 1989 as SkyDome, Toronto's ballpark was the first stadium anywhere with a fully retractable motorized roof — an 11,000-tonne engineering feat that still opens in about 20 minutes.

The 2019 Toronto Raptors

The Raptors' 2019 title run included the first Game 7 buzzer-beater in NBA playoff history and ended with the largest championship parade crowd Toronto has ever seen.

The Blue Jays' Back-to-Back World Series

The 1992 and 1993 Toronto Blue Jays remain the only team based outside the United States to ever win a World Series — and Joe Carter's walk-off home run to clinch the second one is still one of the most replayed moments in Canadian sports history.

1967: The Maple Leafs' Last Stanley Cup

The oldest roster ever to win the Stanley Cup did it in the final season of the NHL's Original Six era — and no Maple Leafs team has managed it since.

Woodbine Racetrack

Home to the Queen's Plate — North America's oldest continuously run horse race, going back to 1860 — Woodbine Racetrack in Etobicoke was built as part of a deliberate 1950s plan to consolidate Toronto-area horse racing into one world-class venue.

The Toronto Argonauts

The Argonauts football club began as an offshoot of a rowing club — which is why a Canadian football team ended up with a name that has nothing to do with football.

How the Blue Jays Got Their Name

Toronto's baseball team was awarded to a group that included CIBC and Labatt Breweries in 1976 — and the winning name from a 30,000-entry contest owed at least as much to a beer brand as to the bird.

The Toronto Arenas

The NHL was founded in 1917 partly to freeze out a Toronto owner — and the improvised replacement team it created won the Stanley Cup anyway, in a franchise line that runs unbroken to today's Maple Leafs.

Conn Smythe

After being fired by the expansion New York Rangers in 1926, Toronto-born Conn Smythe bought a bankrupt local team, renamed it the Maple Leafs, and ran it for 34 years — building Maple Leaf Gardens along the way.

Maple Leaf Gardens

Construction began June 1, 1931; the arena opened five months later. It hosted the Leafs for 68 years, the Beatles twice, and now houses a Loblaws under the same roof as a university hockey rink.

Toronto FC

When MLS awarded Toronto an expansion franchise in 2005, the league had never had a team outside the United States. BMO Field was purpose-built to give it one.

The Toronto Rock

Relocated from Hamilton in 1999 and installed at Maple Leaf Gardens, the Toronto Rock won the National Lacrosse League championship in their first season in the city — and never really stopped winning.

The Toronto Marlies

The Maple Leafs' AHL development team spent decades in New Brunswick, St. Catharines and Newfoundland before landing in Toronto — and taking its name from the storied junior club Conn Smythe bought in 1927.

The 1976 Canada Cup

The first tournament to put NHL professionals against the Soviets and Europe's best crowned Canada champion — with the final's opening game at Maple Leaf Gardens, and Bobby Orr playing what proved to be his last great hockey.

The Vince Carter Era

Before the 2019 championship, there was Vince Carter — a Rookie of the Year, the greatest Slam Dunk Contest performance ever, and a trade in 2004 that split Raptors fans for a decade.

Scotiabank Arena

Built on the site of a 1938 postal building, the arena that opened as Air Canada Centre in February 1999 hosted a hockey game, a basketball game, and a Tragically Hip concert on three consecutive nights.

The Honda Indy Toronto

Toronto has hosted an IndyCar street race on a temporary circuit around Exhibition Place on and off since 1986, closing city streets each July for one of Canada's few major open-wheel racing events.

Northern Dancer

Bred at E.P. Taylor's Windfields Farm north of Toronto and considered too small to sell, Northern Dancer won the 1964 Kentucky Derby and went on to become the most influential sire in modern thoroughbred racing.

Harold Ballard

Harold Ballard controlled the Toronto Maple Leafs from the early 1970s until his death in 1990, running the team through a mix of showmanship, feuds, and decisions that turned much of Toronto against him.

The Founding of the Toronto Raptors

Awarded an NBA expansion franchise in 1994, Toronto's new team took its name from Jurassic Park's velociraptors, part of a deliberately kid-friendly, marketable brand built from scratch.

Exhibition Stadium

Before Rogers Centre, the Toronto Blue Jays played their first twelve seasons at Exhibition Stadium, an open-air multi-purpose venue at Exhibition Place notorious for wind, cold, and flocks of gulls.

Varsity Stadium

Once a large multi-sport stadium that hosted Grey Cup finals, the University of Toronto's Varsity Stadium was demolished and rebuilt smaller in the 2000s to fit modern campus needs.

The Coca-Cola Coliseum

Built in 1922 as a livestock arena for the CNE's agricultural exhibitions, the Coliseum was reconfigured for hockey in the 2000s and became home to the Toronto Marlies.

The Hockey Hall of Fame

The Hockey Hall of Fame moved into a converted 1885 Bank of Montreal building in downtown Toronto in 1993, and now displays the actual Stanley Cup inside a former bank vault.

Balmy Beach

The Balmy Beach Club, founded in 1905 at Toronto's eastern beaches, built a reputation for rugby and rowing championships and a long-running, informal soap-box derby tradition.

Toronto FC's 2017 Treble

Toronto FC won the Canadian Championship, the Supporters' Shield, and the MLS Cup in a single 2017 season, a treble no other Major League Soccer team has matched.

The Toronto Sceptres

The Toronto Sceptres began play in the Professional Women's Hockey League's inaugural 2023-24 season, after decades of fragile, short-lived leagues failed to give women's hockey a stable pro home.

The Toronto Wolfpack

Founded in 2017, the Toronto Wolfpack became the first transatlantic professional sports team, playing in Britain's rugby league system before folding in 2020.

Food & Drink

Toronto Food Culture

Toronto's food scene isn't one cuisine with some diversity mixed in — it's genuinely built from over 200 cuisines. Here's what that actually looks like on the ground.

Toronto's Craft Beer Scene

A city that drank mostly big-brand beer for most of the 20th century now supports more than 70 independent breweries — the modern craft scene traces back to a single 1985 opening.

Toronto's Coffee Culture

Toronto's independent café scene took off precisely when the big chains stumbled — a 2008–09 recession that hit Starbucks hard cleared space for a wave of Toronto-grown, barista-driven coffee shops.

Toronto's Cocktail & Distillery Scene

The same Victorian-era industrial site that once housed the British Empire's largest distillery is now home to some of the city's best bars — and a new generation of small-batch Toronto distilleries.

St. Lawrence Market

Founded in 1803 on the site of Toronto's original town hall, St. Lawrence Market was named the world's best food market by National Geographic in 2012 — and farmers have been setting up in its North Market building every Saturday for more than 200 years.

The Peameal Bacon Sandwich

Cured pork loin, grilled and stacked on a soft roll — the peameal bacon sandwich traces straight back to a 19th-century Toronto meatpacking empire that helped give the city its "Hogtown" nickname.

Toronto's Pizza History

Toronto's pizza story runs through two distinct threads: the city's first pizzeria, opened by an Italian immigrant family with a New York connection, and the founding of one of Canada's biggest pizza chains.

Little Portugal's Bakeries

Family bakeries along Dundas Street West, anchored around custard tarts and Portuguese pastries, trace back to waves of Portuguese immigrants who reshaped this stretch of the city starting in the 1950s.

The Jamaican Patty in Toronto

A flaky, curry-yellow pastry that arrived with Caribbean immigration became one of Toronto's most ordinary foods — ordinary enough that a 1985 regulatory fight over its name became a genuine national story.

Tim Hortons

Tim Horton won four Stanley Cups as a Toronto Maple Leaf. The coffee-and-donut chain that carries his name wasn't founded in Toronto at all — it opened in Hamilton, 65 kilometres away.

Toronto's Chinese Food and Dim Sum Scene

Toronto's Chinese food geography shifted dramatically over a century — from a displaced downtown Chinatown to Spadina Avenue, and later to a wave of suburban banquet halls that made Markham a Chinese-food destination in its own right.

Steam Whistle Brewing

Founded in 2000 by three former employees of a large brewery that had just been shut down, Steam Whistle set up inside the historic John Street Roundhouse and helped kick off Toronto's craft beer movement.

Pizza Pizza

Founded in Toronto in 1967, Pizza Pizza grew into one of Canada's largest pizza chains, its jingle-friendly phone number becoming one of the most recognizable pieces of advertising in Canadian history.

The Ontario Food Terminal

Opened in 1954, the Ontario Food Terminal is a provincially owned wholesale produce market that quietly supplies a large share of the fresh fruit and vegetables sold across Toronto and Ontario.

Toronto's Craft Distilleries

Ontario's licensing rules kept small distillers largely locked out of the province for decades, until regulatory changes around 2013 let a new wave of Toronto craft distilleries open.

Toronto's Food Truck Scene

Toronto's street food carts and trucks were, for decades, legally barred from parking within 50 metres of a restaurant — a rule finally relaxed only after years of lobbying by vendors.

Arts, Media & Culture

Toronto Media & Entertainment

Toronto has been a major film and TV production centre since the late 1970s — and, separately, the launchpad for some of the most influential music of the streaming era.

Toronto Art & Public Art

One of North America's largest art museums, and — a few blocks away — one of the most photographed legal graffiti strips in the country.

Toronto's Comedy Scene

A Chicago import that put down roots in an old firehouse in 1973 turned into one of the most influential comedy pipelines in North America — Toronto's Second City and SCTV.

Toronto's Newspapers

Canada's national newspaper industry is disproportionately headquartered in Toronto — including one paper whose roots trace back to 1844, and a lost-jobs labour dispute that accidentally created another.

Toronto Broadcasting History

Toronto's broadcasting industry produced a genuine world-first in 1927, reinvented local television in 1972, and gave Canada its own answer to MTV in 1984 — a throughline most people don't realize connects three completely different eras.

Toronto's Performing Arts

Canada's first purpose-built opera house opened in 2006 after a 23-year campaign — home to both the Canadian Opera Company and the National Ballet of Canada, alongside a symphony orchestra founded in 1922.

Toronto's Video Game Industry

Montreal and Vancouver are Canada's better-known game-development hubs, but Toronto has quietly built both a major studio under Jade Raymond and one of the country's most influential independent scenes.

OCAD University

Canada's oldest art and design school shares a park with the AGO — and its newest building looks like a giant tabletop lifted on brightly coloured pencil-shaped legs.

How Toronto Popularized the Board Game Café

Snakes & Lattes opened on Bloor Street in 2010 and is widely credited — even if not literally the very first of its kind — with kicking off the board game café boom that later spread across North America, Europe and East Asia.

Toronto's Literary Scene

Home to Margaret Atwood and Canada's largest, longest-running literary festival — plus, for decades, a store that genuinely called itself the World's Biggest Bookstore and largely meant it.

The Group of Seven

Canada's most influential group of landscape painters first exhibited together in Toronto in 1920 — and the couple who later assembled the definitive collection of their work started by buying paintings directly from the artists themselves.

TIFF Bell Lightbox

Toronto International Film Festival's permanent home opened in 2010 on land donated by director Ivan Reitman — and construction crews digging the foundation found artifacts from a hospital that stood on the site nearly two centuries earlier.

The Canadian Broadcasting Centre

CBC's English-language broadcasting empire runs out of a single 13-storey Toronto building — a Philip Johnson-designed complex named partly in honour of one of Canada's most respected broadcast journalists.

Speakers Corner

In 1990, Citytv put a video booth on a Toronto sidewalk and let anyone say anything into it for a dollar. It became one of the strangest and most genuinely democratic experiments in Canadian television history.

The MuchMusic Video Awards

For nearly three decades, the MMVAs turned a stretch of Queen Street West into an open-air concert every June — until the channel that hosted them stopped making music television altogether.

The Canadian Screen Awards

Canada used to hand out separate awards for film and television. In 2013, the two merged into one Toronto ceremony — a genuine consolidation of the country's screen-industry recognition into a single night.

The Atkinson Principles

Joseph Atkinson ran the Toronto Star for nearly 50 years and built it into an explicitly progressive newspaper. The values he set down are still cited as the paper's guiding principles today.

TVO

Ontario built its own public broadcaster in 1970 with a genuinely narrow mandate — education, not entertainment — and became the first full-time educational television station in the entire country.

IMAX's First Permanent Theatre

The technology traces back to Montreal's Expo 67, but the first-ever permanent IMAX theatre opened inside the Cinesphere at Toronto's Ontario Place in 1971 — making it a genuine Toronto landmark in the format's history.

Toronto's Jazz Scene History

From the 1940s through the 1970s, Toronto's jazz life ran through a handful of Yonge Street clubs that hosted Billie Holiday, Miles Davis and Dizzy Gillespie — and doubled as a proving ground for the city's Black Canadian musicians.

The Musicians of 1960s Yorkville

Our Yorkville neighbourhood guide covers the coffeehouse scene broadly. This is about the specific musicians it launched — and why a few blocks of Toronto rooming houses became a genuine music-industry proving ground.

The Most Legendary Concerts at Massey Hall

Our Massey Hall guide covers the venue's history. This is about three specific nights that became legendary recordings — a half-empty 1953 bebop summit, a 1971 solo Neil Young show, and Gordon Lightfoot's decades-long residency.

Toronto Hip-Hop Before Drake

Maestro Fresh Wes, Dream Warriors, Choclair and Kardinal Offishall built a genuine Toronto hip-hop identity — Caribbean-inflected, internationally recognized — for roughly twenty years before Drake's 2011 breakthrough.

Toronto's Classical Music Scene Beyond the TSO

A purpose-built opera house, one of the world's most respected period-instrument orchestras, and the conservatory that trained Glenn Gould — Toronto's classical music scene runs well past the Toronto Symphony Orchestra.

Harbourfront Centre

In 1972, the federal government expropriated 40 hectares of industrial waterfront land and handed it to a Crown corporation with an open-ended mandate: turn it into public cultural space.

The Design Exchange

The building at 234 Bay Street was one of the most technologically advanced stock exchanges in the world when it opened in 1937 — and became a design institution once the traders moved out.

The Gardiner Museum

George Gardiner started collecting ceramics in 1976 just to furnish his home. Eight years later, his collection had grown into the first museum in North America devoted exclusively to ceramic art.

The Bata Shoe Museum

Sonja Bata started collecting shoes while travelling for her family's shoe-manufacturing business in the 1940s. Her collection eventually needed its own museum — designed to look, from the outside, like a giant stylized shoebox.

Roy Thomson Hall

Pierre Trudeau chaired the fundraising committee. The Thomson family put up $4.5 million of the $57 million cost. What resulted was one of Toronto's most recognizable pieces of modern architecture.

David Cronenberg's Toronto

Born and raised in Toronto, David Cronenberg built a decades-long filmmaking career largely shooting in and around the city that helped define the modern Toronto film industry.

The Palais Royale

Opened in 1922 as part of the Sunnyside waterfront scene, the Palais Royale hosted big bands for generations and survived the demolition of the amusement park that once surrounded it.

The Fox Theatre

Opened in 1914 in the Beaches neighbourhood, the Fox Theatre has run continuously as a cinema for over a century, making it Toronto's oldest operating movie theatre.

The Revue Cinema

Opened in 1912 in Roncesvalles, the Revue Cinema faced closure in 2006 before a community campaign turned it into a non-profit, volunteer-supported repertory theatre run for the neighbourhood by the neighbourhood.

The Paradise Theatre

Opened in 1937, the Paradise Theatre on Bloor Street fell into serious disrepair by the 2000s before a lengthy restoration reopened it as a modern cinema and event venue behind its original Art Moderne facade.

The Second City Toronto

Opened in 1973 as a Canadian outpost of Chicago's famous improv theatre, Second City Toronto trained generations of performers who went on to SCTV, Saturday Night Live, and Hollywood.

Factory Theatre

Founded in 1970 with a mandate to produce Canadian plays exclusively, Factory Theatre helped launch a generation of homegrown playwriting at a time when Canadian stages leaned almost entirely on imported work.

Theatre Passe Muraille

Founded in 1968, Theatre Passe Muraille pioneered a collective creation process for Canadian theatre, developing shows through group improvisation and research rather than a single author's script.

Nightwood Theatre

Founded in 1979 by a group of women in Canadian theatre frustrated by how few female-driven stories and creators got produced, Nightwood Theatre became a lasting institution dedicated to women's work.

Buddies in Bad Times Theatre

Founded in 1979, Buddies in Bad Times Theatre built itself into the largest queer theatre company in the world, giving LGBTQ playwrights and performers a stage at a time when few others would.

Toronto Dance Theatre

Founded in 1968 by three former Graham technique dancers, Toronto Dance Theatre helped establish contemporary modern dance as a serious art form in a city where it barely existed as an institution before.

Pierre Berton

Pierre Berton spent decades as a Toronto newspaperman, magazine editor, television personality and prolific popular historian, becoming one of the most recognizable figures in Canadian media.

Peter Gzowski

Peter Gzowski hosted CBC Radio's Morningside from Toronto for almost 20 years, becoming one of the most trusted and recognizable voices in Canadian broadcasting.

Barbara Frum

Foster Hewitt

Rush

Geddy Lee and Alex Lifeson met as teenagers in Willowdale, North York, and built Rush into one of the best-selling rock bands in Canadian history without ever leaving Toronto as their base.

Broken Social Scene

Formed in Toronto in 1999 with a rotating cast of up to twenty members, Broken Social Scene became the centre of a wider community of Toronto indie bands in the early 2000s.

Barenaked Ladies

Ed Robertson and Steven Page started busking together in Scarborough in the late 1980s, and Barenaked Ladies went on to write the theme song for The Big Bang Theory decades later.

Maestro Fresh Wes

Toronto rapper Wes Williams released 'Let Your Backbone Slide' in 1989, the first Canadian hip hop single to go gold, opening a path Toronto's later hip hop generation would follow.

The Globe and Mail

George Brown's Globe, founded in 1844, merged with the Mail and Empire in 1936 to create the paper widely treated as Canada's national newspaper of record.

The Toronto Sun

When the Toronto Telegram shut down in 1971, a group of its former staff launched the tabloid Toronto Sun almost overnight, building it into the city's brash alternative to the broadsheets.

The Toronto Symphony Orchestra

Founded in 1922 and long anchored by conductor Sir Ernest MacMillan, the TSO moved from Massey Hall to become the founding resident company of Roy Thomson Hall in 1982.

The Canadian Opera Company

Founded in 1950, the Canadian Opera Company performed for over half a century in shared and retrofitted venues before Toronto finally got a purpose-built opera house in 2006.

The National Ballet of Canada

Celia Franca founded the National Ballet of Canada in Toronto in 1951, and decades later principal dancer Karen Kain became its artistic director, closing a loop from the company's founding generation.

Tarragon Theatre

Founded in 1970 by Bill Glassco in a converted Annex-area factory, Tarragon Theatre became one of the central venues for developing new Canadian playwriting.

Soulpepper Theatre

Founded in 1998 by a group of Toronto actors, Soulpepper built a year-round classical repertory company inside the then-newly redeveloped Distillery District.

Canadian Stage

Formed from the 1988 merger of Toronto Free Theatre and CentreStage, Canadian Stage is best known for its Dream in High Park summer Shakespeare series.

Michael Ondaatje's Toronto

Ondaatje's 1987 novel In the Skin of a Lion turned the building of the Bloor Viaduct and the R.C. Harris plant into a story about the immigrant workers official history left out.

Robertson Davies and Massey College

As founding Master of the University of Toronto's Massey College from 1963 to 1981, novelist Robertson Davies shaped a deliberately old-fashioned graduate community and wrote his Deptford Trilogy there.

CFRB

Ted Rogers Sr. launched CFRB in 1927 to give Toronto listeners something to hear on the batteryless radios his invention made possible — the station later became NewsTalk 1010.

CHUM Radio

After switching to a Top 40 format in 1957, 1050 CHUM became Toronto's dominant rock and roll station and printed the CHUM Chart, a weekly hit-parade sheet handed out at record stores for three decades.

The CBC Broadcasting Centre

After decades spread across converted buildings around the city, the CBC's English-language Toronto operations moved into a single purpose-built headquarters on Front Street in 1992.

Glenn Gould's Toronto

Born in the Beaches in 1932 and buried at Mount Pleasant Cemetery in 1982, Glenn Gould kept Toronto as his base for his entire career, working largely through the CBC.

From the largest Caribbean carnival in North America to one of the world's biggest film festivals, Toronto's event calendar is packed almost year-round.

Toronto's end-of-summer ritual: an agricultural fair turned mega-event, running every year for 18 days leading up to Labour Day since 1879.

Toronto Zoo & the Ontario Science Centre

One of the largest zoos in the world by area, and a beloved Brutalist science museum the province abruptly closed in 2024 — two very different fates for two of Toronto's classic family attractions.

A Modernist waterfront park that drew millions of visitors a year in the 1970s, closed its main attractions in 2012, and is now the site of one of Toronto's most contested current redevelopment fights.

Toronto's Winter Festivals

TIFF: The Toronto International Film Festival

Founded to fix what its creators saw as the cold, exclusive treatment festival guests got elsewhere, TIFF let ordinary moviegoers buy tickets alongside press and industry — and became one of the world's most prominent film festivals.

Luminato Festival

The Toronto Christmas Market

Cavalcade of Lights

What began as a single-night lighting ceremony to showcase Toronto's brand-new City Hall has run every year since 1967, growing into a month of free skating parties and fireworks at Nathan Phillips Square.

Pride Toronto

Our bathhouse raids guide covers the 1981 police raids that sparked Toronto's first Pride march. This is the story of what that single march grew into: a multi-day festival now drawing millions of people every June.

Winterlicious and Summerlicious

The city built two prix-fixe dining promotions around the two slowest stretches of the restaurant calendar — and they've grown into an eight-million-meal, $353-million tradition.

Caribana

Launched in 1967 by Toronto's Caribbean community to mark Canada's centennial, Caribana grew into one of the largest Caribbean carnival festivals outside the Caribbean itself.

The Santa Claus Parade

Eaton's launched the Toronto Santa Claus Parade in 1905 to draw shoppers downtown, and the annual event outlived the department store that created it by decades, becoming one of the oldest continuously running parades of its kind anywhere.

The Royal Agricultural Winter Fair

Held annually at Exhibition Place since 1922, the Royal Agricultural Winter Fair brings livestock competitions, horse shows and farm exhibits into the heart of one of Canada's largest cities.

Fan Expo Canada

What began as a small comic book convention grew into Fan Expo Canada, one of the largest pop culture and comic conventions in North America, filling the Metro Toronto Convention Centre every summer.

Doors Open Toronto

Launched in 2000, Doors Open Toronto gives the public free access to buildings normally closed to visitors, from historic churches to modern office towers, for one weekend each spring.

The Beaches International Jazz Festival

Launched in the 1990s, the Beaches International Jazz Festival turns Queen Street East and the boardwalk into a series of free outdoor jazz stages each July, a lower-key counterpart to Toronto's main jazz festival downtown.

Centreville Amusement Park

Opened in 1967 on the Toronto Islands, Centreville was designed as a deliberately old-fashioned, low-key amusement park scaled to young children, reachable only by ferry.

The Toronto Dragon Boat Race Festival

Launched in 1989, the Toronto International Dragon Boat Race Festival brought an ancient Chinese paddling tradition to the Toronto Islands and grew into one of the largest dragon boat festivals outside Asia.

Winter Stations

Since 2015, an annual design competition has turned the Beaches' otherwise-idle lifeguard stands into temporary public art installations through the depths of a Toronto winter.

Pedestrian Sundays

Since 2003, Kensington Market has closed its streets to cars on select summer Sundays, turning the neighbourhood into an impromptu street festival that helped define its counterculture identity.

Business & Economy

Toronto's Economy: Bay Street

Toronto has been Canada's financial capital since the 1970s, when Bay Street took that role from Montreal's St. James Street — here's what actually sits in the Financial District.

Toronto's Tech Scene

Toronto has quietly become one of North America's largest tech hubs — enough that it's earned the nickname "Silicon Valley North," built on a deep AI research bench and a startup ecosystem that grew out of the same university corridor that discovered insulin.

Toronto's Shopping Malls

Toronto's two biggest malls anchor very different stories — one holds Canada's highest sales per square foot of any mall, the other is home to a sculpture that produced a landmark moral-rights lawsuit.

Canada's Big Banks

Only one of Canada's major banks actually started in Toronto — the rest migrated in from Montreal and Halifax over the following century, before Bay Street became the inevitable place for all of them to be.

Eaton's & Simpson's

For most of the 20th century, two department stores facing each other across Queen Street defined downtown Toronto shopping — one of them started the world's oldest Santa Claus parade in 1905, the other outlasted it by exactly zero years.

Rogers Communications

Ted Rogers Jr. borrowed $85,000 to buy a single FM station in 1960 and built it into one of Canada's largest media and telecom companies — continuing, in a real sense, his father's own broadcasting legacy.

De Havilland Canada

One of the most famous bush planes ever built came out of a Toronto aircraft plant that, at its wartime peak, employed 7,000 people — many of them women — at Downsview.

The Hudson's Bay Company

One of the oldest companies in the world sold most of what's now Canada to Canada itself in 1869, became a Toronto department-store fixture for over a century — and stopped operating as a retailer entirely in 2025.

Canadian Tire

Brothers J.W. and A.J. Billes pooled $1,800 in 1922 to buy a tire depot. A century later, the company they built is a national institution — and its loyalty coupons are informally accepted currency at businesses that have nothing to do with tires.

Shoppers Drug Mart

Murray Koffler opened a single Toronto pharmacy in 1962 with a name blended from two other brands. Fifty-two years later, Canada's largest grocery company bought the chain he built in the country's largest-ever retail acquisition.

Loblaws

In 1919, two Toronto grocers let customers pick goods off the shelf themselves — a radical idea at the time. Three decades later, the Weston family quietly bought control of the chain they founded.

Toronto's "Big Five" Banks

Bay Street looks uniformly Toronto — but two of Canada's five biggest banks still legally file as Montreal companies, and one as a Halifax one, even as all five run their real operations from a few blocks of downtown Toronto.

Bombardier's Toronto-Area Operations

The snowmobile company from Quebec became a major Toronto-region presence only through acquisition — buying the historic De Havilland Canada plant and the Thunder Bay factory that still builds GO Transit's rail cars.

Magna International

Frank Stronach opened a one-man tool-and-die shop in Toronto in 1957. By 2008, the company he built into Magna was the largest automotive-parts supplier in North America.

Shopify's Toronto Expansion

Founded in Ottawa, Shopify built its second-biggest presence in Toronto — nine floors of a King Street tower, backed by a half-billion-dollar investment commitment.

Wattpad

Allen Lau and Ivan Yuen started a story-sharing platform out of a Toronto garage in 2006. Fifteen years later, a South Korean tech giant bought it.

Wealthsimple

Michael Katchen built a spreadsheet to help Silicon Valley colleagues manage their investments. The idea he took from that became one of Toronto's biggest fintech success stories.

Ubisoft Toronto

Ontario's government put up $263 million to help a French video-game giant open a Toronto studio — part of a bet that helped make the city home to more than 300 game development studios today.

Bay Street

Bay Street grew from an ordinary downtown Toronto road into the informal name for Canada's entire financial industry, the way Wall Street stands in for American finance.

Manulife

Founded in Toronto in 1887 as the Manufacturers Life Insurance Company, Manulife grew into one of the largest insurance and financial services companies in the world.

The Robert Simpson Company

Robert Simpson's small dry goods store, founded in 1872, survived two major fires before growing into Simpsons, the department store chain that eventually merged into Eaton's chief rival before its own 1991 closure.

The Consumers' Gas Company

Founded in 1848 to light Toronto's streets with gas lamps, the Consumers' Gas Company grew into a major regional utility before eventually becoming part of what is now Enbridge Gas.

E.P. Taylor

E.P. Taylor built Canadian Breweries into a national conglomerate from Toronto, then turned his Windfields Farm into one of the most successful thoroughbred breeding operations in racing history.

The Toronto Board of Trade

Founded in 1845 by the city's merchants, the Toronto Board of Trade grew into the central voice of the city's business community, shaping decades of civic infrastructure and economic policy debates.

Peter Munk

Peter Munk fled Hungary as a teenager in 1944, eventually building Barrick Gold into the world's largest gold mining company from a Toronto base, and donating hundreds of millions to the city's institutions.

The Toronto Stock Exchange

Founded in 1861, the Toronto Stock Exchange grew into Canada's largest exchange and was among the first in the world to move entirely off a physical trading floor to electronic trading, in 1997.

Honest Ed's

Ed Mirvish's discount emporium opened in 1948 with hand-painted signs and outrageous sale gimmicks, growing into a Bloor and Bathurst landmark that closed and was demolished in 2016 and 2018.

Massey Manufacturing

Hart Massey's farm machinery company grew into Massey-Harris, one of Canada's largest manufacturers — and the family fortune it produced built both Massey Hall and Massey College.

Timothy Eaton

Timothy Eaton opened his first Toronto dry goods store in 1869 on the then-radical promise of fixed prices, cash only, and money-back guarantees, building the T. Eaton Co. into a national institution.

Second Cup

Founded in Toronto in 1975 selling whole bean coffee, Second Cup became one of Canada's largest coffee chains and one of the first to popularize specialty coffee culture in the country.

Pinewood Toronto Studios

Opened in 2008 on former industrial land in the city's east end, Pinewood Toronto Studios became a major soundstage complex anchoring Toronto's growing film and television production industry.

The Spadina Garment District

Jewish immigrants settled around Spadina Avenue in the early 20th century and built it into the centre of Canada's clothing manufacturing industry, known simply as 'the rag trade.'

Indigo Books & Music

Founded in Toronto in 1996 by Heather Reisman, Indigo grew into Canada's dominant bookstore chain by merging with its rival Chapters in 2001.

Canada Goose

Founded in Toronto in 1957 as a small outerwear manufacturer, Canada Goose spent decades supplying Arctic researchers before becoming a globally recognized luxury fashion brand.

Cineplex

Cineplex opened its first multi-screen theatre at the Eaton Centre in 1979, and later mergers with rival chains made it Canada's dominant movie theatre operator.

Roots

Michael Budman and Don Green founded Roots in Toronto in 1973 after searching for a comfortable negative-heel shoe, later making the company famous for outfitting Canada's Olympic teams.

Famous People From Toronto

A city that produced a Nobel Prize-winning medical discovery and a generation-defining rap career has a longer list of notable natives than its music-scene reputation alone suggests.

Is Toronto Safe?

Toronto's reputation is safer than its headlines sometimes suggest — the actual crime statistics put it well below the Canadian national average and among the safest big cities in North America.

Religious Diversity in Toronto

Toronto's 19th-century religious makeup was overwhelmingly Protestant. The modern city holds functioning places of worship for nearly every major world religion — often inside buildings originally built for a completely different faith.

Homelessness in Toronto

Toronto runs one of the largest municipal shelter systems in the country — under sustained pressure for years, and backed by a genuine network of city and community-run supports.

A massive security operation, a small number of Black Bloc property-destruction incidents, and a mass police response that resulted in over 1,100 arrests — most never leading to charges.

Marshall McLuhan's Toronto

The media theorist behind "the medium is the message" and "the global village" wasn't born in Toronto — but his entire career, and the ideas that made him famous, were built at the University of Toronto.

Northrop Frye's Victoria College

The literary critic behind Anatomy of Criticism entered Victoria College at 17 and never really left — a career spanning student, professor, department chair, principal and chancellor, almost entirely on one Toronto campus.

Margaret Atwood's Toronto

Atwood moved to Toronto at six years old, studied under Northrop Frye at Victoria College, and has lived in the Annex neighbourhood since the mid-1980s — making her arguably the district's most famous literary resident.

Norman Jewison

Born in the Beaches to a general-store family, Norman Jewison went on to direct In the Heat of the Night, Fiddler on the Roof and Moonstruck — then came back to Toronto to build a training ground for the next generation.

Michael Ondaatje's Toronto Literary Life

The author of The English Patient built his literary career from a Toronto small press and a bilingual college campus — a quieter Toronto story than the Booker Prize and nine-Oscar film that made him famous.

E.J. Pratt

Newfoundland-born, Toronto-made — E.J. Pratt taught at Victoria College for 46 years while becoming, in the words of one encyclopedia, the foremost Canadian poet of the century's first half.

Little Italy, College Street

Toronto's first Italian community wasn't on College Street at all — it started in the crowded downtown Ward, and moved west as postwar immigration swelled Toronto's Italian population into one of the largest outside Italy.

Kensington Market's Jewish History

Before it was the multicultural market it is today, Kensington was known as "the Jewish Market" — Victorian rowhouses converted into shops by families who'd moved west out of an overcrowded downtown ward.

Vietnamese Toronto

The fall of Saigon in 1975 and the 'boat people' crisis that followed brought one of the largest refugee resettlement waves in Canadian history to Toronto — built substantially through ordinary citizens sponsoring families directly.

South Asian Toronto

A 1908 law effectively banned South Asian immigration to Canada without naming race explicitly. It took nearly six decades and a complete overhaul of immigration policy for Toronto to become home to one of the largest South Asian populations outside South Asia.

Black Toronto History

Toronto's Black community traces to Underground Railroad refugees who built the city's first cab business and helped fund its churches — a population that later shrank for decades before Caribbean immigration transformed it after 1967.

Frederick Banting

Banting's famous late-night idea came in London, Ontario. The actual discovery — the lab work, the dogs, the summer of failed experiments that finally worked — happened at the University of Toronto.

John Candy

Born in Newmarket and raised in East York, Candy trained at Toronto's Second City alongside Catherine O'Hara and Eugene Levy before SCTV made his characters famous across North America.

Catherine O'Hara

O'Hara grew up in Etobicoke and got her start doing coat check at Toronto's Second City — a job that turned into a stage role once Gilda Radner left for Saturday Night Live.

Mike Myers

Long before Saturday Night Live, a young Mike Myers was already doing Wayne Campbell's slouch and voice — based on older kids he watched at parties growing up in Scarborough.

Keanu Reeves

Born in Beirut, Reeves grew up entirely in Toronto — playing goalie at a private Catholic school and acting on a north-Toronto stage years before his first film role.

Jim Carrey

Before Hollywood, Carrey's family lost their home and took custodial work at a Scarborough factory — the teenager worked overnight shifts there before attending school by day, and started standup at Yuk Yuk's not long after.

The 2018 Yonge Street Van Attack

On April 23, 2018, a rented van was deliberately driven onto sidewalks along Yonge Street in North York, killing ten people. The driver was convicted of first-degree murder in 2021.

The Boyd Gang

Edwin Alonzo Boyd's gang robbed banks across Toronto in the early 1950s and twice escaped the Don Jail, drawing a strange mix of public fascination and manhunt-level police attention.

Toronto's Ismaili Community

A significant wave of Toronto's Ismaili Muslim community arrived as refugees after Idi Amin expelled Uganda's Asian population in 1972, building one of the largest Ismaili communities outside South Asia and East Africa.

The British Methodist Episcopal Church

Formed in the 1850s after Black congregants split from a Methodist denomination that wouldn't treat them as full equals, the British Methodist Episcopal Church became a central institution for Black Torontonians.

Ukrainian Toronto

Ukrainian immigration to Toronto came in distinct waves across more than a century, building institutions concentrated around Bloor West Village that remain central to one of Canada's largest Ukrainian communities.

Japanese Canadian Toronto

Most of Toronto's Japanese Canadian community traces back to the forced postwar dispersal of Japanese Canadians out of British Columbia, after years of wartime internment stripped them of homes and property on the coast.

The YMCA in Toronto

Toronto's YMCA, established in the 1860s, grew from an evangelical young men's organization into a major provider of recreation, housing and social services across the city.

Jane Jacobs

Jane Jacobs moved to Toronto in 1968 already famous for fighting New York's Robert Moses, and immediately threw herself into blocking the Spadina Expressway — a fight that reshaped how the city thought about its own downtown.

Mary Pickford

Born Gladys Smith in downtown Toronto in 1892, Mary Pickford became one of the most famous women in the world during silent film's golden age, and a co-founder of United Artists.

Rick Moranis

Before Ghostbusters and Honey, I Shrunk the Kids, Toronto-born Rick Moranis worked Toronto radio and stand-up before joining SCTV, where his Bob McKenzie character became a genuine pop-culture phenomenon.

Samantha Bee

Toronto-born Samantha Bee spent twelve years as the longest-serving correspondent on The Daily Show before becoming the first woman to host her own weekly American late-night show.

Sarah Polley

Sarah Polley started as a Toronto child actress before turning to directing in her thirties, eventually winning an Academy Award for adapting Women Talking in 2023.

June Callwood

Alongside a long career as a Toronto journalist, June Callwood helped found dozens of social service organizations, including Canada's first hospice for people dying of AIDS.

Emily Stowe

Refused admission to Canadian medical schools for being a woman, Emily Stowe trained in the United States and practiced medicine in Toronto for over a decade before finally being licensed in Ontario in 1880.

Toronto's Raccoons

Toronto's dense urban raccoon population, drawn by abundant food waste and few natural predators, forced the city to design and redesign its green compost bins specifically to outsmart them.

The 519

Housed in a former municipal building at 519 Church Street since the 1970s, The 519 grew from a general neighbourhood community centre into a central institution of Toronto's LGBTQ community.

Glad Day Bookshop

Glad Day opened in 1970 when carrying queer literature could mean police raids and obscenity charges, and it has outlasted nearly every bookstore of its era to become a Church Street institution.

The Christie Pits Riot

On August 16, 1933, a swastika unfurled at a baseball game at Willowvale Park drew thousands into a night of street fighting, with Jewish and Italian youth largely fighting on the same side.

The Yonge Street Riot of 1992

A May 4, 1992 protest against the Rodney King verdict and a local police shooting escalated into looting along Yonge Street, and led to the province's landmark Stephen Lewis report on racism.

Science, Health & Education

Toronto's Universities

Three major universities, three very different founding stories — and one of them is where the academic research behind this entire dictionary actually comes from.

Toronto's Medical History

One of the most consequential medical discoveries of the 20th century happened in a University of Toronto lab in the summer of 1921 — and Toronto's hospitals have stayed near the centre of Canadian medicine ever since.

The 2003 SARS Outbreak in Toronto

Toronto had the largest SARS outbreak outside Asia in 2003 — serious enough that the World Health Organization briefly advised against travel to the city, and serious enough that its recovery needed the largest ticketed concert in Canadian history.

Toronto's Hospitals

Beyond the insulin story covered in our medical history guide, two of Toronto's major hospitals carry genuinely distinct founding stories — one built specifically for Second World War veterans, the other founded because Jewish physicians couldn't get hired anywhere else in the city.

How Stem Cells Were Discovered

James Till and Ernest McCulloch weren't trying to discover stem cells in 1961 — they were studying radiation damage in mice. What they found instead reshaped modern medicine.

Connaught Labs

A University of Toronto lab founded in 1914 to make diphtheria antitoxin became, within a few decades, the sole Canadian source of insulin and a global supplier for one of the largest vaccine trials in history.

The Vector Institute

Toronto's dedicated AI research institute opened in 2017 with the "godfather of AI" as a founding advisor. Seven years later, he won the Nobel Prize in Physics for the work that made the whole field possible.

The Cystic Fibrosis Gene Discovery

A Toronto research team identified the gene behind cystic fibrosis in 1989 — a discovery still described as one of the most significant in the history of human genetics.

The Toronto District School Board

Toronto's public schools were run by six separate boards until a 1997 provincial law dissolved them all — creating, on the same day the city itself amalgamated, one of the largest school boards in North America.

George Brown College

Chartered in 1966 as one of Ontario's new applied-arts colleges, George Brown absorbed two existing Toronto trades institutes before growing into a three-campus institution spanning Kensington Market, Casa Loma and the waterfront.

Seneca, Centennial and Humber Colleges

Ontario had no province-wide network of applied, career-focused colleges before 1965. A single bill from Education Minister Bill Davis created the whole system — and Toronto got three of the first colleges within a year.

French-Language Education in Toronto

Ontario banned French-language teaching in most schools in 1912. It took nearly ninety years, a formal apology, and a Charter right to build the French-language school boards that serve Toronto's Francophone community today.

Toronto's Specialized Arts High Schools

Etobicoke School of the Arts, Claude Watson, and Rosedale Heights all trace back to individual school boards in the 1980s and '90s — giving Toronto an unusually dense network of standalone public arts high schools.

Adult ESL and Newcomer Education in Toronto

Behind Toronto's reputation as a city of immigrants sits a large, federally funded infrastructure of free adult English classes — delivered locally through school boards and community agencies, at a scale most residents never see.

Mount Sinai Hospital

In the early 1920s, almost no Toronto hospital would hire a Jewish doctor. A women's charitable group opened one that would — and it grew into a major research hospital on University Avenue.

Princess Margaret Cancer Centre

A princess cut the ribbon twice, three decades apart, as this hospital grew from a single 1958 building into the largest comprehensive cancer program in Canada.

The Ontario Science Centre

Raymond Moriyama's celebrated Don Valley building opened for Canada's 1967 centennial celebrations and ran for 55 years — until the province closed it in 2024, with a smaller replacement planned for Ontario Place by 2028.

MaRS Discovery District

The old Toronto General Hospital building where insulin was first clinically tested became, nearly a century later, the anchor of a 1.5-million-square-foot innovation hub.

The David Dunlap Observatory

A widow bought a farm in Richmond Hill and donated it to the University of Toronto, memorializing her astronomer husband. The telescope built there confirmed one of the 20th century's biggest astrophysics discoveries.

The Toronto Catholic District School Board

Ontario's constitutionally protected separate school system gave Toronto's Catholic community its own publicly funded board, tracing back to 19th-century guarantees for Catholic education.

University Avenue's Hospital Row

University Avenue's unusually wide, landscaped boulevard was built to give Toronto's cluster of major teaching hospitals — Toronto General, SickKids, Mount Sinai and others — a suitably grand civic setting.

Sunnybrook Health Sciences Centre

Sunnybrook opened in 1948 specifically to care for Canadian Second World War veterans, and grew over subsequent decades into one of the country's largest hospitals and trauma centres.

The Rotman School of Management

The University of Toronto's business school existed for decades before a 1997 donation from businessman Joseph Rotman funded a rebuild that turned it into one of Canada's top-ranked programs.

Hart House

Opened in 1919 as a gift from the Massey family, Hart House gave University of Toronto students a Gothic Revival hub for athletics, theatre, art and debate modelled on British collegiate life.

Convocation Hall

Opened in 1907 after fire destroyed the University of Toronto's previous convocation space, Convocation Hall's distinctive dome has hosted graduations, first-year lectures, and touring concerts for over a century.

CAMH

The Centre for Addiction and Mental Health formed from a 1998 merger of four Toronto institutions, on grounds that once housed the notorious Provincial Lunatic Asylum and its surrounding wall.

Toronto Western Hospital

Toronto Western Hospital was founded in 1896 by a group of homeopathic and other physicians shut out of the city's mainstream medical institutions, and later became a leading neurosurgery centre.

The Hospital for Sick Children

SickKids opened in 1875 with just six beds, and its research wing later became the site of the 1921 discovery that infant formula needed vitamin D — one of several major medical breakthroughs made there.

Toronto General Hospital

Toronto General Hospital traces its roots to a small early-19th-century hospital, and later became the site of the first successful insulin treatment and Canada's first heart transplant.

Women's College Hospital

Women's College Hospital grew out of a medical school founded in 1883 because Toronto's existing schools refused to properly admit and train female medical students.

Robarts Library

The University of Toronto's massive Brutalist central library, opened in 1973, is famous for its distinctive triangular floor plan and a nickname — Fort Book — that says as much about its critics as its architecture.

Ryerson to Toronto Metropolitan University

Founded in 1948 as a technical institute named for education reformer Egerton Ryerson, the university dropped his name in 2022 over his role shaping Canada's residential school system.
