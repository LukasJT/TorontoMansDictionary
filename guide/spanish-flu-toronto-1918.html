The 1918 Flu Pandemic in Toronto

Toronto's medical officer of health closed schools, theatres and churches within weeks of the virus arriving in the fall of 1918, in a pandemic that killed healthy young adults at an unusual rate.

Advertisement

Toronto's experience with the 1918-1919 influenza pandemic, often called the Spanish flu though it did not originate in Spain, came in waves overlapping the final months of the First World War. The virus reached the city by early autumn 1918, likely carried by troops and returning soldiers moving through military camps and rail lines, and by early October cases were climbing fast enough that Toronto's Medical Officer of Health, Dr. Charles Hastings, ordered the closure of schools, theatres, churches and other places of public assembly. Hastings, already known for aggressive reforms around tenement housing and milk safety, ran one of the more assertive municipal responses in Ontario — though, as everywhere, there was no vaccine, no antiviral treatment and no reliable way to isolate the infectious before symptoms appeared.

Overwhelmed hospitals

Toronto's hospitals were overwhelmed within weeks, with emergency wards set up in halls and public buildings to handle the overflow. Organizations like the Victorian Order of Nurses became central to the response, sending nurses into private homes because hospital capacity couldn't keep pace. A single reliable death toll for the city isn't settled in the available record — estimates run into the low thousands across several waves, within a provincial Ontario toll usually put above 8,000 and a Canadian national toll estimated near 50,000. Unlike a typical flu season, the pandemic disproportionately killed healthy adults in their twenties and thirties, a pattern reflected in Toronto's own mortality records.
