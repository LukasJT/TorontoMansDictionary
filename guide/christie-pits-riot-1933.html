The Christie Pits Riot

On August 16, 1933, a swastika unfurled at a baseball game at Willowvale Park drew thousands into a night of street fighting, with Jewish and Italian youth largely fighting on the same side.

Advertisement

On the evening of August 16, 1933, a baseball game at Willowvale Park — now known as Christie Pits — between a mostly Jewish team from the Harbord Playground and a mostly Anglo-Protestant team turned into the worst outbreak of ethnic violence in Toronto's history. Tensions had built for weeks: a group calling themselves the Swastika Club had been displaying swastika flags at the Beaches and other public spaces that summer to intimidate Jewish Torontonians, echoing the rise of Nazism in Germany that same year. At the end of the game, club members unfurled a blanket painted with a swastika and shouted "Heil Hitler," and the underlying tension broke into open fighting that drew in several thousand people as word spread through the surrounding Jewish and Italian immigrant neighbourhoods.

Six hours of street fighting

The violence spread from the park into the surrounding streets, reportedly continuing for roughly six hours into the early morning, with dozens of serious injuries reported and no deaths recorded. Jewish and Italian young men from the west-end Ward and Little Italy largely fought on the same side against the group associated with the Swastika Club — an alliance often cited as an early moment of solidarity between the two communities. The riot pushed city council toward restricting the public display of hate symbols and remains the reference point for Toronto's worst single episode of ethnic violence, documented in detail in Cyril Levitt and William Shaffir's 1987 study The Riot at Christie Pits.
