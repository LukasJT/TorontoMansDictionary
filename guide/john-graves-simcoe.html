He never intended York to become the permanent capital

John Graves Simcoe

Upper Canada's first Lieutenant Governor founded York in 1793 as a defensible, temporary capital, choosing the site primarily for military reasons rather than any long-term vision of the city Toronto would become.

John Graves Simcoe, a British Army officer who had commanded loyalist forces during the American Revolutionary War, became the first Lieutenant Governor of the newly created province of Upper Canada in 1791. He founded the town of York in 1793 on the north shore of Lake Ontario, choosing the site primarily for its well-protected natural harbour, seeing it as a defensible naval and military position rather than as a capital he expected to remain permanent.

A reluctant capital, and a lasting legacy

Simcoe actually preferred London, further inland along the Thames River, as Upper Canada's eventual capital, viewing York as a temporary seat of government chosen mainly for wartime security against potential American attack. His broader legacy includes signing the Act Against Slavery in 1793, a measure that began the gradual abolition of slavery in Upper Canada decades before Britain's empire-wide abolition, making the province an early legal foothold for the Underground Railroad routes that later brought freedom seekers to Toronto. Simcoe left Upper Canada in 1796, but the town he founded as a defensive outpost grew, against his own expectations, into the country's largest city.
